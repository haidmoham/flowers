# Visual direction research

Research date: 2026-09-04. This is a proposal for the private Flowers artwork. It is not visible site copy.

## Direction

Make a drawing that keeps the evidence of wanting to get it right. Use warm paper, fragile graphite, and translucent pigment that does not quite meet its contour. Keep one coral flower as the emotional center. Let pale blue, butter yellow, and small wine accents gather around it. The most consequential change is the relation between line and color. A pink palette alone cannot make the current rendered surface intimate.

Confidence: high that this follows the supplied clip and feedback. Whether the result feels romantic still needs visual judgment in motion.

## Direct reference observations

I inspected the extracted contact sheet and the full frame at 11.5 seconds from `C:/Users/haidm/Desktop/demos/480p_flowers.mov`. The contact sheet starts with a completed bouquet, resets, and then draws. The opening hold is not part of the drawing arc.

- Fine, slightly angular contour loops extend well past the colored areas. Pigment and outline disagree in size and position. That disagreement is central to the look.
- The flowers overlap at a small central gathering. Individual blooms are not isolated display objects. Much of the frame remains paper.
- Stems and leaves arrive before the flower contours. The contours establish a fragile structure before saturated centers gather inside it.
- Color concentrates at throats and fades outward. Many petal ends remain almost empty. There is no continuous lit rim.
- The final image retains incomplete contours, crossing marks, and thin green stems. It does not resolve into a clean silhouette.

The original `sketch.js` supports these observations: warm paper, a large coral hero, staggered flower delays, and lighter peripheral flowers. The current `astral/garden.js` has a dark ground and a parametric surface renderer. That architecture can render beautiful objects, but it puts pressure on this brief to become a material demonstration. This is an interpretation of the code and reference, not a claim about all dark artwork.

## Three precedents

### Odilon Redon, Wildflowers, c. 1905

The National Gallery of Art identifies the medium as pastel on brown wove paper coated with sawdust. I inspected its collection image. Coral and yellow blooms carry weight, while smaller blue flowers and thin branches loosen the perimeter. Paper remains visible between marks. Borrow the uneven distribution of saturation and the active role of the ground. Do not borrow the vase or turn the bouquet into a historical reproduction. [Collection record and image](https://www.nga.gov/artworks/46299-wildflowers).

### Auguste Rodin, Nude female figure holding left foot, 1900–1912

The Met identifies graphite and watercolor and describes a fleeting, unstable pose. I inspected its public collection image. Repeated contour searches remain visible around the head and body. Broad translucent color crosses those searches without tidying them. The relevant precedent is the visible separation of observation, correction, and wash. A flower can retain a second attempt at a tip without becoming a wobbling cartoon. [Collection record and image](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/339720).

### Cy Twombly, Untitled, 1968

I inspected the image in RISD Museum's discussion of expressive line. The loops vary in pressure, spacing, direction, and completion. Their repetition records a gesture; it does not resemble uniform random displacement. Borrow this distinction: choose where a hand hesitates, speeds up, or tries again. Do not import the dense scribble field or any writing. [RISD Museum, Drawing Outside the Lines](https://risdmuseum.org/manual/271_drawing_outside_the_lines).

## Concrete direction for the web study

Use a warm ivory ground near the original, with fixed low-contrast fibers and a few broad paper variations. Avoid an animated grain layer. Give the bouquet roughly half the available short dimension, with an asymmetric airy perimeter and stems exiting the lower edge. Preserve a dense center; do not replace the bouquet with three isolated specimens.

Keep contour coverage and wash coverage independent. Let color occupy roughly the inner half to two-thirds of most petals. Allow a few washes to cross an edge. Use one darker pigment deposit at a throat, a few granulated patches, and one or two pale unpainted petals. Replace the glossy rim and uniform surface mesh with these differences.

Author imperfections by feature. Give the coral hero one brief tip hesitation and a faint alternate tip. Give one companion a broken returning contour. Leave a peripheral bud partly unresolved. Most lines should be confident. If every line hesitates, nervousness becomes a filter.

Use an approximately 13–15 second drawing arc. Suggested score: first stem within 0.3 seconds; stems and leaves through 3 seconds; central contours from 3–6 seconds; coral pigment from 5.5–8 seconds; companions from 7–11 seconds; peripheral marks from 10–13 seconds; one small final correction near 14 seconds. Keep brief overlaps, but limit simultaneous high-contrast events. Use local pauses of about 0.1–0.25 seconds inside a stroke, not a long blank prelude.

After the final mark, allow stillness. Persistent shimmer risks making the marks feel like a screen effect. If touch remains, use a restrained local pigment response and let it settle. Do not add instructions, titles, buttons, or a mandatory interaction.

## Review criteria

Inspect the drawing at 0.5, 2, 4, 7, 10, and 14 seconds. At each stage, a viewer should be able to identify the active gesture. Inspect the final frame at phone size. Fine contours must survive, while paper remains visible inside the petals. Compare a paused frame with the supplied clip: if the new work reads as a glossy object with a roughness overlay, the material change has not gone far enough.
